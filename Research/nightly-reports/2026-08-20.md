## 1. The failure — `mainline()` enumerates 121 million world-lines

`worldlines.mainline()` finds the argmax world-line by full Cartesian enumeration
(`worldlines.py:638`). Its own docstring states the bound it works under:

> "The exact argmax world-line by full enumeration (≤ a few thousand cells)"

That bound is wrong by five orders of magnitude.

```
axes/positions   T 5 · K 4 · A 7 · C 8 · R 6 · D 4 · S 5 · P 5 · E 5 · L 6 · G 6
combinations     120,960,000
one mainline()   93.3 minutes  (5600.10s, direct wall-clock)
emit()           3.1 hours — it calls mainline() TWICE
```

**Three independent readings, agreeing inside 9%.** In the order they became available:

```
throughput sample   19,722 combos/s × 121M cells        102 min   projected, 7-9% HIGH
exemplars(k=5)      5706.16s = one call + 5 world-lines  95.1 min  wall-clock
mainline() direct   5600.10s                             93.3 min  wall-clock
```

The two wall-clock readings bracket correctly — the bare call is the smaller, and `exemplars`
adds its five world-line instantiations on top. The projection was the outlier and the high one,
inflated by sampling while the stalled emit was still competing for the CPU. **The figures above
are the measured ones**; this report carried the 102-minute projection until the probes landed at
13:03, which is why the changelog records both and says which is which.

**The negative control matters as much as the number.** Both probes also timed
`bands(reg, n=100)` at 0.02–0.03s. That is what rules the rest of the emit *out* — the sampler,
the capability paths, the band envelopes — rather than leaving them merely unaccused while the
arithmetic pointed elsewhere. One function, called twice, was the entire outage.

Supporting readings: `joint_probability` costs 49.7 µs on an idle machine over 20,000 calls, and
a scaling sweep across 4→7 axes shows the per-combination cost itself rising with axis count
(13.7 → 28.4 µs) because the function loops over every axis — so the growth is worse than the
combination count alone.

The second call is not needed for a second purpose: `emit()` computes it at
`forecast_emit.py:200`, and `worldlines.exemplars()` recomputes the identical value at
`worldlines.py:668`.

**The cost is multiplicative in axes, and two axes landed in the last nine hours.** r7 (axis L,
committed 02:16 today) multiplied the space by 6; r8 (axis G, still uncommitted in the working
tree) multiplied it by 6 again — 36× since yesterday. Yesterday's whole chain committed eleven
minutes after it started. Tonight's emit ran 38 minutes at 100% CPU without reaching its first
write, and I killed it after measuring what it was doing. Every other stage in the chain is fast;
the sampler itself draws 19,000 world-lines in about six seconds.

**Tomorrow's run will fail identically.** This is not a transient.

I did not retry. The task file asks for one retry after diagnosis, and the diagnosis here is a
measurement rather than an inference: an unchanged retry would occupy the machine for 3.1 hours
and arrive at the same place. Reporting instead.

**The fix is August's call, because the cheap version changes what the mainline means.**
`joint_probability` factorises over the registry order — each axis's tilt depends only on which
parent positions earlier axes already chose (`worldlines.py:610-626`) — so the exact same argmax
is reachable by variable elimination over parent sets, with no change to the result. That is a
real rewrite of a function whose output is the single most visible object in the artefact, and I
am not shipping it unattended. Caching the duplicated call is exact and free, and halves 3.1
hours to 1.6 — still unusable on its own.

## Postscript 2 — 13:15, independent verification of what is live

Three strands of evidence now stand behind the published forecast, with different failure modes.
The Forecaster's **48 comparisons** test the *algorithm* against enumeration on small spaces. The
**exhaustive 120,960,000-cell run** — still enumerating at the time of writing — tests the same
claim on the seed registry; it enumerates `axes.REGISTRY`, so it answers "branch and bound equals
enumeration", not "the published line is optimal on the space the emit used". That second question

**The published mainline, fetched from the live host and checked on the widened registry:**

```
{T:T2, K:K1, A:A2, C:C1, R:R4, D:D2, S:S1, P:P3, E:E3, L:L4, G:G4}   p = 7.353175929e-06

probability recomputed        exact to 10 digits
1-coordinate swaps               50 neighbours   HELD (best 6.696e-06)

ensemble draws              300,000 draws        HELD — the best draw IS the published line
```

A strict local maximum at radius 2, unbeaten by 300,000 draws from the distribution itself. Not
proof of global optimality: 300,000 is 0.25% of the space, and radius 2 says nothing about a
distant better line.

**A trap for anyone spot-checking a published figure.** My first attempt recomputed the
probability against `axes.REGISTRY` and got `9.033818372e-06` against the published
`7.353175929e-06` — a 23% gap, indistinguishable at a glance from a stale or corrupted artefact.
The fault was the check's: `emit()` runs on `widened_registry(weights, grounding["widen"])`, with
